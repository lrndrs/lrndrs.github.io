## Research statement

I study how past changes in ice-sheet meltwater have shaped ocean circulation and
climate, using speleothems — cave mineral deposits — as high-resolution terrestrial
archives. My work centres on the Last Deglaciation (roughly 20,000–10,000 years
ago), a period of abrupt climate transitions driven by massive meltwater discharge
from the Northern Hemisphere ice sheets into the North Atlantic. During my PhD
(*"Of Caves and Currents: Tracing Meltwater in Iberian Speleothems of the Last
Deglaciation"*, ETH Zürich, 2021–2025), I developed a conservative dye-tracer
approach that links Iberian speleothem geochemistry to the freshening of North
Atlantic surface waters and the associated slowdown of deep convection — connecting
a cave-based proxy record directly to large-scale ocean circulation change (AMOC).
This thesis was awarded the **ETH Zürich Silver Medal**.

I care about proxy data being reusable beyond the paper it was
published in, which is part of why I contribute to
[SISAL](https://sisal-db.com/) (Speleothem Isotopes Synthesis and AnaLysis), the
global speleothem stable isotope and trace element database, where I sit on the
PAGES steering committee.

More broadly, I'm interested in where palaeoclimate science meets data science and
climate modelling — building reproducible, script-based analysis pipelines, and
using isotope-enabled and forcing-sensitivity model experiments to test hypotheses
that proxy records alone can't resolve.

Before moving into climate geology, I trained and worked as a sound engineer and
sound designer (MA Tonmeister, Universität für Musik und Darstellende Kunst Wien);
signal, noise, and listening carefully to messy time series are themes that, in
hindsight, connect both halves of my career.
